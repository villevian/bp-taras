Who wants to go back to the office?

Six years after the big move home, the debate isn’t over

In 2020, millions of people were sent home with a laptop and a promise: ‘It’s only for a few weeks.’ Six years later, the debate continues. Office attendance is rising again: workers around the world now spend about 3.2 days a week in the office, up from 2.7 in 2024. Yet in a 2026 survey of over 4,000 US workers, only 4% said they wanted to work in the office full-time. So what is really wrong with working from home? I spoke to Marta Lis, a project manager who has led a distributed team from her kitchen table for five years.

1

Loneliness is the problem that is mentioned most often in online remote-work communities. In one large survey, one in five remote workers named it as their biggest challenge. ‘Some weeks I’m only spoken to on Slack,’ Marta says. ‘I didn’t realise how much I needed small talk by the coffee machine.’ Younger employees, who are still building their professional networks, are affected most.

2

Remote workers often feel invisible – and they may be right. Research shows that people who work from home are promoted less often than their office colleagues, even when their results are just as good. Managers simply notice the people they see every day. ‘Last year a new team lead was chosen,’ Marta remembers. ‘He was in the office every day. I delivered more projects, but nobody saw it.’

3

When your laptop lives on the kitchen table, work never really ends. Many remote workers say they work longer hours, not shorter ones. Messages are sent at 10 p.m., and people feel they have to answer. ‘Nobody tells you to reply in the evening,’ Marta explains, ‘but if you don’t, you feel guilty.’ For many people, this is how burnout starts.

4

Surprisingly, many extra meetings are created by managers who are worried about losing touch with their teams. A quick check-in is added here, a status call there, and soon the whole day is filled with video calls. ‘On my worst day, I was invited to seven meetings,’ Marta says. ‘When was I supposed to do the actual work?’

5

The last problem is more practical. Friends and family often think that people who work from home have the day off. ‘My neighbour asks me to wait for her deliveries,’ Marta laughs. Remote workers also pay more for electricity and heating, and these costs are rarely covered by employers.

So, is the office the answer? Not completely. Most workers still don’t want to go back five days a week, and many say they are more productive at home. But the problems above are real, and they are not solved by a faster laptop or another app. Marta now goes to the office three days a week. ‘I didn’t want to give up working from home,’ she says. ‘I just wanted to be seen again.’ Perhaps that is the real lesson of 2026: the best place to work is not home or the office, but a smart mix of both.

Present Simple and Past Simple passive

Present Simple passive: am / is / are + past participle → Tasks are assigned in Jira.

Past Simple passive: was / were + past participle → The deadline was moved last week.

by + agent (only when it’s important who did it) → The report was approved by the client.
