The skills that survive AI

When AI writes the first version of the code, what do employers look for?

In 2026, about half of all tech job ads ask for AI skills. So is the future only for people who can build AI agents? Not exactly. When AI writes the first version of the code, employers start looking for something else: transferable skills – skills that can be applied to a range of tasks and roles.

‘A tool you learn today can be old news next year,’ says Lena Park, head of engineering at a fintech start-up in Berlin. ‘So when I hire, I look for people who can think outside the box and who can set goals they can actually achieve. Flexibility matters too – our tech stack changes every year.’

Critical thinking is now more important, not less. AI tools make mistakes that look very convincing. A good engineer checks the output, asks ‘Why?’ and doesn’t accept the first answer. Problem-solving is the same: AI can suggest ten solutions, but someone has to choose the right one and explain the choice to the team.

Then there is teamwork. Most products are built by distributed teams, so you need to be a good team player and work well with other people – people you may never meet in person. Your communication skills are tested every day: in person, on video calls, in writing on Slack and in docs, one-to-one with your manager, and in large groups when you demo your work to the whole company.

Lena also lists the ‘human’ qualities: honesty, integrity and being genuine. ‘If you broke the build, say so. If AI wrote the code, say so,’ she says. She wants people who are passionate about the product, motivated and hard-working, with a can-do attitude – and the determination to keep going when a release goes wrong.

Her advice? First, collect skills you can take with you from one job, tool or team to the next. Second, be a team player – nobody ships a product alone. And third, remember that employers look at the person behind the CV and the GitHub profile.
