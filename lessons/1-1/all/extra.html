One developer, three clients

More and more engineers build a portfolio career instead of a single job. What does it take?

Mykola is a backend developer from Lviv. He doesn’t have one employer – he has three clients: a logistics start-up in the Netherlands, a US agency that builds e-commerce sites, and a Polish fintech company. He works with all of them as a contractor, and his contracts are renewed every six months. This kind of working life is called a portfolio career.

‘The money is better than in my old full-time job, but that’s not the main reason,’ he says. ‘I learn something new on every project, and if one client stops working with me, I still have two others.’

The difficult part, he explains, is not the code. ‘Every client has a different tech stack, different tools and a different culture. So the most important skill is onboarding fast: in the first week I read the docs, ask a lot of questions and deliver something small.’

Communication is the next big skill. His clients are in three time zones, so he works mostly asynchronously: he writes a short update on Slack every evening and keeps his availability clear in his calendar. He has also learned to manage expectations. ‘If a client asks for a new feature, I agree the scope and the deadline first. Saying “not this week, but next week” is much better than missing a deadline.’

Then there is the business side. Mykola sends an invoice to each client at the end of the month, keeps track of his hours and reviews his rate once a year. Between projects he works on a side project, which he adds to his portfolio on GitHub. ‘New clients rarely read my CV,’ he says. ‘They look at what I’ve built and they ask my old clients about me.’

Is it for everyone? ‘No,’ he admits. ‘There’s no paid holiday, and some months are quiet. You need to be organised, disciplined and good with people. But if you like variety, it’s a great way to work.’

1. Mykola has one full-time employer and two side projects.
2. For him, learning on every project is more important than the money.
3. He says the hardest part of the job is writing the code.
4. In the first week with a new client, he tries to deliver something small.
5. He prefers to promise a quick deadline, even if he can’t meet it.
6. New clients usually decide after looking at his work and talking to old clients.
