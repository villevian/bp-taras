The skills that survive AI

When AI writes the first draft of the code, what do employers actually look for?

In 2026, roughly half of all tech job ads mention AI skills, and many developers are asking themselves an uncomfortable question: if a machine can write the code, what am I being paid for? According to hiring managers, the answer is not to compete with AI, but to focus on what it can’t do. Increasingly, employers are looking for transferable skills – skills that can be applied to a range of tasks and roles, no matter which tools the team happens to use.

‘A framework you master today may be old news by next year,’ says Lena Park, who leads an engineering team at a fintech start-up in Berlin. ‘So when I’m hiring, I’m less interested in a long list of technologies. I want people who can think outside the box and who can set goals that are ambitious but realistic. Flexibility matters too, because our tech stack changes almost every year.’

Surprisingly, critical thinking has become more important since AI tools arrived, not less. AI assistants make mistakes that look extremely convincing, so a good engineer double-checks the output, asks ‘Why?’ and never accepts the first answer just because it works. Problem-solving works in a similar way: AI may suggest ten possible solutions, but someone still has to choose the right one and justify that choice to the rest of the team.

Then there is teamwork. Most products today are built by distributed teams spread across several time zones, which means you need to be a good team player who works well with other people – including people you may never meet in person. Your communication skills are tested every day: in person, on video calls, in writing on Slack and in documentation, one-to-one with your manager, and in large groups when you demo your work to the whole company.

Lena also values the ‘human’ qualities that are hard to automate: honesty, integrity and being genuine. ‘If you broke the build, admit it. If AI wrote half of your code, say so,’ she explains. She is looking for people who are passionate about the product, motivated and hard-working, with a can-do attitude – and enough determination to keep going when a release goes badly wrong.

So what is her advice for people who want to stay employable? First, collect skills that you can take with you from one job, tool or team to the next. Second, be a team player, because nobody ships a successful product alone. And finally, remember that employers don’t just read your CV or scroll through your GitHub profile – they are looking at the person behind them.
